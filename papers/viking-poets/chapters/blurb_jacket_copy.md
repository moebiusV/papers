# Jacket copy / blurb

**Tagline:** The beauty of the baud, the poetry of the process

**Descriptive alternate:** From ancient epic poems to modern AI: the technology of mind

A tease, not a summary. Keep paragraph one; end on the open question.

---

## Primary

A skald composing in tenth-century Iceland and an engineer at Bell Labs in 1950
were working on the same problem, and they reached the same answer: structure
is redundancy, and redundancy is how a signal survives a channel that wants to
corrupt it. One of them worked in kennings, alliteration, and meter. The other
worked in parity bits.

The skald's channel was human memory, a poem carried intact across centuries of
imperfect recall; the engineer's was a noisy wire. Same fight against loss,
different substrate. And the skalds were only one workshop among many: the
scribes who counted every letter of the Hebrew Bible, the reciters who kept the
Vedas word-perfect for three thousand years, and others besides were building
information technologies to hold knowledge against forgetting, corruption, and
forgery.

Now a machine writes with perfect fluency and no idea when its own memory has
invented something, while the field rediscovers these old defenses one patch at
a time. If *Gödel, Escher, Bach* and *Hackers & Painters* are on your shelf, you
have felt this convergence coming. The question the book leaves you turning
over: what can the information technologies of the ancient world teach modern AI?

## Shorter alternate

A tenth-century Viking skald and a 1950s Bell Labs engineer were solving the
same problem: how a signal survives a channel that wants to corrupt it. One
answered in kennings and meter, the other in parity bits. The skald's channel
was human memory; the engineer's a noisy wire. The ancient world was full of
such workshops, whole information technologies built to hold knowledge against
forgetting and forgery. Now a machine writes with perfect fluency and no idea
when it is lying. So what can those old technologies teach modern AI?
