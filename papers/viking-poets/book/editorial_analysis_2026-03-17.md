# Editorial Analysis: *What the Viking Poets Knew*

Date: 2026-03-17

---

## Overall Verdict

Not quite publication-ready. Close — the argument is rigorous, the footnotes are excellent, and the best passages (Ch. 1's shop class section, Ch. 2's SSD/Torah comparison, Ch. 3's kenning-as-Hamming-code) are genuinely strong. But there's an identifiable stratum of AI-generated text that sits on top of an otherwise good book, and it needs to come out before this goes to a copy editor. It reads like a first-rate draft that hasn't been fully inhabited by the author's voice yet.

---

## The Em-Dash Problem

The book uses spaced em-dashes throughout — the European style — and the frequency is the AI tell, not the style itself.

**Acceptable (parenthetical clarification where other punctuation would be awkward):**
- "the SSD — a solid-state drive, used here as shorthand for fast, precise external storage; the external memory that we'll discuss in Chapter 2 — is one form of external knowledge store" (Ch. 1) — two em-dashes enclosing a long parenthetical, fine.
- "mirror neurons — the neural cells that fire both when you perform an action and when you observe someone else performing it — generate a dim echo" (Ch. 1) — same structure, same verdict.

**Should be periods or colons:**
- "large language model — an AI system that generates text by predicting the most likely next word in a sequence, trained on a vast corpus of human writing — generates a confident, fluent, authoritative statement" (Intro) — this is a definition that belongs in a footnote. The em-dash parenthetical interrupts the sentence so severely that by the time you get to the verb "generates" you've forgotten the subject.
- "he's also a programmer who builds translation pipelines and studies Torah — the Hebrew scriptures — which is how this book came about — put it this way" (Ch. 1) — three em-dashes in one sentence. Worst instance in the book. The cheesemaker's full introduction was already given in the Introduction; this sentence is trying to re-introduce him with a footnote crammed into the prose.
- "A person who re-reads a text without monitoring their internal state is re-processing mechanically — running the same input through the same model and getting approximately the same output." (Ch. 5) — colon.
- "It is the incorruptible manuscript — which is the subject of our final chapter." (Ch. 4 closing) — weak chapter-ending, and the em-dash makes it worse. Cut the second clause entirely: "It is the incorruptible manuscript." Full stop. Let the chapter title do the rest.

**Used for drama where a period would be stronger:**
Multiple instances in Ch. 5 where a period would land harder.

## AI Tropes Beyond the Em-Dash

**"It's worth [verbing]" constructions — all cut:**
- "it's worth pausing here to note something that the modern world has largely forgotten" (Ch. 3)
- "it's worth examining in detail" (Ch. 3)
- "it's worth dwelling on because the dynamics are identical" (Ch. 4)
- "it's worth naming because it's directly relevant" (Ch. 4)

These are hedging phrases. They signal that the author is uncertain whether the next point merits inclusion. If it merits inclusion, include it without the apology.

**"At its core" (Ch. 2):** "At its core, citation exists because you need to know WHO said something." One of the most AI-generated phrases in existence. Cut it.

**"This matters for the AI architecture in a way that isn't obvious at first" (Ch. 1):** Every sentence in this book matters for the AI architecture. The self-justifying clause is unnecessary.

**"By the way" (Ch. 3 opener):** "By the way, there's a reason the ancient oral traditions encoded their texts the way they did." This is the opening of a chapter section. "By the way" signals a digression. This is not a digression — it's the thesis of the chapter. Starting a chapter with "By the way" is the single worst line in the book.

**"It sort of works" (Ch. 2):** Aiming for breeziness, landing on vagueness. "It partly works" is sharper. Or just: "Here's how it works, and here's where it fails."

**"This is the entire argument of this book in miniature" (Ch. 3):** The author explaining their own structure to the reader as if the reader couldn't follow it. Cut it; the preceding paragraph is already perfect.

**Caps for emphasis ("The catchiness IS the error correction," Ch. 3):** Italics, not caps. This is a typesetting/AI tell throughout.

## Grammar and Usage

1. Ch. 1: "Someone built a working CPU in Minecraft once." The trailing "once" is awkward. "Someone once built" is cleaner.

2. Ch. 1: "The wall can't get balance wrong" — slightly strained. "The wall doesn't have a vestibular system to get wrong" is more precise.

3. Ch. 4: "The fear driving this is usually disproportionate to reality but real to those who feel it." The tail ("but real to those who feel it") is self-evident — all fears are real to those who feel them. The sentence is trying to be fair-minded but blunts its own point.

4. Ch. 5: "Whether a silicon system can develop genuine self-monitoring — rather than a statistical approximation of it — is an open question. But the architectural point stands regardless." "The architectural point stands regardless" should be the start of a new sentence, not buried after "But."

---

## Idiomatic Issues

**"The AI field mostly ignored Brooks" (Ch. 1):** Rhythmically soft. The following line ("It was easier to scale the simulation") carries the weight but they should be one sentence.

**"The cover-up is always worse than the crime" (Ch. 4):** True, but it's a cliché landing softly in an otherwise rigorous section. The surrounding argument is more original than this phrase.

---

## Flow

**Chapter-level:** Excellent. The bridge endings are one of the book's genuine strengths:

- Ch. 1: "Every civilization that encountered this problem arrived at the same answer. They wrote it down." — strong.
- Ch. 2: "The skalds knew." — perfect. Best ending in the book.
- Ch. 3: "What if the corruption isn't accidental? What if someone is deliberately poisoning the well?" — good.
- Ch. 4: "It is the incorruptible manuscript — which is the subject of our final chapter." — weakest. Cut the second clause.

**Within chapters:** Chapter 5 has a structural problem. It starts as the synthesis chapter (naming the six layers), then pivots into an extended new argument about metacognition and contemplative self-monitoring that runs for five section-breaks. This is new material being introduced in what is supposed to be the convergence chapter. The argument is interesting and belongs in the book, but it belongs somewhere in the development (possibly expanded Ch. 2 or as a separate chapter), not in the chapter that's supposed to be closing. A synthesis chapter should synthesize, not expand.

Chapter 5 is also the most visibly AI-generated chapter. The metacognition section is where the AI tropes cluster most densely.

---

## Rhythm and Internal Meter

The book's rhythm is built on a specific technique: long explanatory sentence or two, then a hammer — a very short declarative that clinches the point. This works beautifully when it works:

- "You can't argue with a table saw." — excellent.
- "The skalds knew." — excellent.
- "The cheese is the arbitration." — excellent.
- "Writing began not as literature but as accounting." — strong.

The rhythm breaks down in Chapter 5, which has more long paragraphs and fewer hammers. The metacognition section reads differently from the rest of the book — longer sentences, more hedging, less concrete.

The tricolon is used well throughout ("the skalds, the Brahmins, the Masoretes"), but in Chapter 5 the "Remove the VM... Remove the sensors... Remove the SSD..." repetition runs to five iterations. The fifth has diminishing returns.

The book's best single sentence: "The transformer is the prefrontal cortex. The VM is the cerebellum. One plans. The other executes." (Ch. 1) — excellent four-beat parallel.

## Chiastic Structure

**Book-level chiasm (intentional and strong):**
- Introduction opens with the skalds.
- Ch. 5 closes: "The question isn't how to build a bigger brain. It's how to give it a body, a notebook, and colleagues who will tell it when it's wrong."
- This echoes the Introduction's "Current AI has built an impressive brain and connected it to nothing."
- The return is clean and satisfying.

**Chapter-level chiasms (not deployed, missed opportunity):**

The book argues that chiastic structure is a "hidden layer of encoding" whose "central element carries the passage's primary emphasis." But the book doesn't use chiasms in its own prose. Given the subject matter, this is a notable absence — talking about the technique without demonstrating it.

The Twain section in Chapter 1 (memory palace → memory walk → driveway pegs → Aboriginal songlines) develops as a deepening spiral but doesn't exploit the structure. It reads as four consecutive examples when it could be framed explicitly as an ABCB'A' movement.

## Poetic Devices

**What's working:**

- *Anaphora*: The "Remove any one of these layers" sequence in Ch. 5 is effective, if slightly overlong.
- *Parallelism*: "The transformer is the prefrontal cortex. The VM is the cerebellum. One plans. The other executes." — best sentence in the book.
- *Kenning (appropriately)*: "brain in a jar" is the book's own kenning, deployed consistently and well.
- *Chiastic return at book level*: the Introduction's "brain... connected to nothing" answered by Ch. 5's "body, a notebook, and colleagues."

**What's not working:**

- *Extended metaphor discipline*: The seed/tree metaphor in the Introduction is set up beautifully ("the seed is not the tree") but then abandoned. It doesn't return in Ch. 5 where it could anchor the synthesis.
- *The "door" metaphor*: "No door to go back and check" (Intro) is a strong, concrete, memorable image — then never returns. Should come back at the end of Ch. 5.
- *Deliberate wordplay*: The book praises prophetic puns as error-correcting devices but the book's own prose doesn't play with language at this level. Missed opportunity given the subject matter.

## Standards Compliance (per book plan's own style standard)

1. "Short paragraphs, concrete before abstract, contractions, sentence fragments for emphasis" — Ch. 5 violates this consistently: longer paragraphs, more abstract than concrete, fewer fragments.

2. "The footnotes are the scholarly layer. The main text is the readable layer. They don't contaminate each other" — violated in three places:
   - The large-language-model definition in the Introduction should be a footnote, not a parenthetical.
   - The "which is how this book came about" aside in Ch. 1 belongs in the Introduction where it already lives.
   - The WHOIS/GDPR explanation in Ch. 4 footnote 10 is excellent precisely because it's where it belongs.

3. "Every person named in the text gets enough introduction to place them" — Nakamoto is identified by pseudonym without being fully placed. The book's own standard calls for "the pseudonymous cryptographer whose 2008 paper founded Bitcoin" or similar.

- "It's worth [verbing]" — all four instances, cut
