Arabia — backed by the Table of Nations itself: Genesis 10:29 lists Ophir among the sons of Joktan, sandwiched between Sheba and Havilah, an Arabian genealogical neighborhood. India — and here the alignments stack in a way the others can't match:



The zoological one is close to dispositive: tukkiyyim. The blue peacock, Pavo cristatus, is native to the Indian subcontinent and nowhere else — not Arabia, not Africa. Provenance of the temple-dedication reading — older and more mainstream than Barker. The chain runs: Moshe Weinfeld's 1981 essay "Sabbath, Temple and the Enthronement of the Lord," which laid out the Genesis 1 ‖ tabernacle-construction parallels (seven divine speeches in Exodus 25–31 matching seven creation days; "God saw all that he had made" ‖ "Moses saw all the work"; blessing and completion formulas nearly verbatim between Genesis 2:1–3 and Exodus 39–40); Jon Levenson's Creation and the Persistence of Evil (1988), which generalized it — temple as microcosm, creation as macro-temple; Gordon Wenham's short paper on Eden's sanctuary symbolism (the keruvim, the eastward entrance, the gold and onyx of Havilah matching priestly materials, and the verb pair of 2:15, le-ʿovdah u-le-shomrah, "to serve and to guard" — used together elsewhere only of Levitical temple duty); and John Walton popularized the whole stack in 2009. Behind all of them, the ANE comparanda: Enuma Elish climaxes in building Esagila for Marduk's rest; the Baal cycle climaxes in Baal's palace; the Gudea cylinders end a temple dedication with the god entering his rest. Deity-rests-in-completed-temple was the standard capstone of a cosmogony across the entire region. Barker is downstream of this and runs it much harder — her distinctive move is reading Genesis 1 as encoding First Temple liturgy and architecture (Day One as the Holy of Holies, the veil as the firmament) rather than the temple as encoding creation. My assessment, two courts as always. Benner's bara as "fill, fatten" — not legitimate as the meaning in 1:27, and the failure mode is instructive. Hebrew has three homonymous b-r-ʾ roots in the lexica: bara I, "create," with God as subject in every occurrence; bara II, "cut down, carve" (Joshua 17:15, 18 — clearing forest; Ezekiel 23:47); and b-r-ʾ III behind bariʾ, "fat" (Pharaoh's fat cows). Benner's method collapses homonyms into one "concrete original meaning" — that's the root fallacy, the assumption that formal identity guarantees semantic unity. You know exactly what this error is, because it's the uncontrolled version of Edenics: pattern-matching on consonants without a null model. The syntax also kills it locally: "fill" is bivalent — you fill X with Y — and bara never takes that construction; it takes a plain direct object (heavens, sea-monsters, man). Van Wolde tried a different revision in 2009 (bara = "separate") and the field stress-tested it against the same syntactic evidence and rejected it. The irony worth keeping: if you wanted a secondary resonance for 1:27, bara II — "carve" — serves your temple reading far better than "fill" ever could: the image carved, then installed. I wouldn't put that in the article as the meaning, but as overtone it points the right direction, whereas Benner's points the wrong one.

Whether the substrate was Minoan, the substrate existed, and Israel entered as appointed stewards over it. That's the text.



And the bronze serpent as healing — here the archaeology hands you something better than the Minoan route, and it runs straight through your own territory: Midian. At Timna, the copper-mining district north of Eilat, the Egyptian Hathor shrine was taken over in the 12th century BCE by Midianites, who converted it to a tent sanctuary — and in that Midianite tent-shrine was found a copper serpent with a gilded head, about five inches long, a votive cult object. Sit the pieces in a row: Moses, trained in Egypt, exiled to Midian, married to a Midianite priest's daughter, in a region whose economy is copper, where the local shrine venerates a copper serpent — and Numbers 21 has him fashion a neḥash neḥoshet, a bronze serpent (the Hebrew is itself a pun, naḥash/neḥoshet, serpent/copper, two words from one sound), for healing, in the wilderness south country, which then survives as a healing cult object in Jerusalem until Hezekiah smashes Nehushtan seven centuries later because Israel "burned incense to it." Egypt supplies the healing-serpent function too: the Horus cippi — stelae of Horus gripping serpents, over which water was poured and drunk against snakebite and disease — are the standard Egyptian magical-medical use of serpent imagery. So the bronze serpent doesn't need a Minoan vector at all: its iconography, its material pun, its healing function, and its later idolatrous drift are all native to the Egypt–Midian copper corridor Moses actually lived in. And that drift — sanctioned artifact to forbidden cult, inside Israel, ending under a reformer's hammer — is, as I said last time, your documented precedent for the whole Eden-memorial thesis: the canon shows you, start to finish, exactly how a true sign degrades into an asherah.
