> *The palest ink is better than the most retentive memory.*
> — Chinese proverb

{{c:id00102}} divided memory into tiers: a small fast working memory, a consolidation store behind it, and a vast slow archive at the bottom, the same hierarchy a computer builds as cache, RAM, and disk, for the same reason, because speed and capacity trade against each other and the fast tier is always the smallest. Every tradition this book treats lives at the slow end. The skalds, the Masoretes, the reciters of the Veda, whose crafts fill the chapters ahead, were solving the archive's problem, how to carry a fixed body of content intact across generations, and there is a practical reason their tier dominates the story while the fast tier gets this one chapter: the slow tier leaves artifacts. A preserved poem, a counted scroll, a chain of named transmitters can be excavated, compared, and studied a thousand years later. The fast tier's work is a thought held for eight seconds and released. It leaves nothing to dig up, and for most of history the craft that grew around it was invisible even to its own practitioners, who would have told you they were just doing the job. Then two things happened. Engineers began building systems in which an operator's working memory was a matter of life and death, so that the craft had to become explicit; and, much later, researchers opened a language model and found the fast tier sitting inside it, built by no one. This chapter is about that craft, on both sides of the skull.

The constraint it answers is the one {{c:id00102}} stated: working memory holds about seven items, give or take, and everything a chain of reasoning needs beyond that must live somewhere slower and be fetched when wanted.[^1] For a bard the constraint never forced a response, because his product was the finished utterance; he reasoned in his head and spoke the result, and nobody needed the middle. Some jobs need the middle. Some jobs *are* the middle, a running state that must be held for hours, updated continuously, shared with colleagues, and never, under any circumstances, dropped. Those are the jobs where the craft of the fast tier became visible.

For most of the twentieth century, the controllers who kept aircraft from colliding did it with strips of paper. One strip per flight: callsign, aircraft type, route, and altitude printed across a band of card, which the controller then annotated by hand as the flight progressed, each cleared altitude and heading written down at the moment it was issued. The strips lived in racks beside the radar screen, arranged in the order that mattered, and the arrangement was itself information: a controller rearranged them as the traffic picture changed, and when he saw a conflict developing that he could not yet resolve, he would cock the strip, tilt it out of alignment in its bay, so that the unsolved problem physically stuck out of the board and went on announcing itself until it was dealt with. The board was not a record of the controller's thinking but the thinking itself, the working memory of the sector moved out of one head and onto a surface where a colleague could read the whole traffic picture at a glance, where a supervisor could audit any decision, where a shift handover consisted of handing over the board, and where a moment's distraction, a sneeze, a phone call, a lapse of attention, could not silently erase a flight from existence. When researchers finally studied the strips closely, in the 1990s, they were sent by administrations that assumed paper was a legacy inefficiency waiting to be automated away, and what they found instead was that the paper was doing cognitive work nobody had ever specified: the writing fixed the clearance in the controller's own memory as he wrote it, the physical handling kept his attention distributed across all his aircraft, and the strip's location and angle carried state that appeared nowhere else in the system.[^2] The move to electronic strips was eventually made, but it took decades, and it was made with the caution of people who had come to understand that they were not replacing stationery; they were performing surgery on the operation's working memory while the patient stayed awake and the aircraft stayed airborne.

The researchers who studied the strips had documented something they could report but not explain: the act of writing a clearance by hand fixed it in the controller's memory in a way that typing the same information into a keyboard did not. The observation sat in the human-factors literature for a quarter century without a mechanism, until a Norwegian neuroscientist closed the gap from an unexpected direction.

Audrey van der Meer runs a brain research lab at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology in Trondheim. In 2024, she and her colleague Ruud van der Weel published a paper that should have changed every classroom on Earth. The experiment was simple. Thirty-six university students sat in a cap studded with 256 EEG sensors while words flashed on a screen. Sometimes they wrote the word by hand on a touchscreen with a digital pen; sometimes they typed the same word on a keyboard. The team recorded brain activity across the full five seconds each word stayed visible, and then they looked at the part of the data most researchers had ignored for years: how different regions of the brain were communicating with each other during the task.

When the students wrote by hand, the brain lit up everywhere at once. The regions responsible for memory, sensory integration, and the encoding of new information fired together in a coordinated pattern that spread across the entire cortex, what neuroscientists call widespread functional connectivity, a brain state in which distant regions are talking to each other in sync. When the same students typed the same word, that pattern collapsed almost completely. Most of the brain went quiet, and the connections between regions that had been alive seconds earlier were nowhere to be found on the EEG. Same word, same brain, same person, two completely different neurological events.[^3]

The reason turned out to be specific and mechanical. Writing by hand is not one motion but a sequence of thousands of tiny micro-movements coordinated with the eyes in real time. Each letter is a different shape that requires the brain to solve a slightly different spatial problem, a small motor-planning puzzle unique to that letter, one that engages the fingers, the wrist, the visual system, and the parietal regions that track position in space all at once. Typing throws all of that away. Every key on a keyboard, regardless of which letter is being pressed, requires the same finger motion. The brain has almost nothing to integrate and almost no problem to solve. Van der Meer stated it plainly: pressing the same key with the same finger over and over does not stimulate the brain in any meaningful way. She added a detail that should worry any parent who handed a child a tablet: children who learn to read and write on screens often cannot reliably tell letters like *b* and *d* apart, not because they cannot see the difference (they can) but because they have never physically felt with their bodies what it takes to produce those letters on a page. The distinction has no foothold in the sensorimotor system that would make it stick.

A decade before van der Meer, two psychologists at Princeton arrived at the same answer using a completely different method with no EEG caps in sight. Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer tested 327 students across three experiments. Half took notes on laptops with the internet disabled; half took notes by hand. Everyone was tested afterward on what they actually understood from the lectures they had watched. The handwriting group won by a wide margin on every question that required conceptual understanding rather than surface recall of facts. The reason was hiding in the transcripts of what the two groups had written down. The laptop students typed almost word for word, capturing more total content but processing almost none of it as they went. The handwriting students physically could not write fast enough to transcribe a lecture in real time, which forced them to listen carefully, decide what mattered, and put it in their own words on the page. That single act of choosing what to keep *was* the learning itself, and the keyboard had quietly skipped the choosing and skipped the learning along with it.[^4]

Two studies, two countries, same answer, arrived at from opposite directions. Van der Meer found the mechanism in the brain: handwriting forces widespread neural integration, and typing does not. Mueller and Oppenheimer found the consequence in the mind: handwriting forces active processing, and typing enables passive transcription. The mechanism and the consequence are two views of the same event. The micro-movements of the hand demand that the brain stay fully online, and a brain that is fully online during encoding produces memories that survive retrieval. A brain that coasts through encoding produces memories that crumble on contact with a test question, or with the next shift change. The controller who wrote a clearance on a flight strip was not merely recording it for colleagues or pinning it to a board where it would survive a distraction. He was forcing his own brain to process the instruction at the depth that makes it permanent. The electronic strip, whatever its other operational virtues, cannot make the same claim.

The controllers arrived at externalization under pressure, but the maneuver is ancient, and the abacus is its oldest refined instrument. The beads of a soroban are working memory rendered tactile: they hold the running state of a calculation, every carry and partial sum, so that the operator's head does not have to, and it is precisely because the head has been relieved of the bookkeeping that a trained operator outruns unaided mental arithmetic by so wide a margin. The instrument is not a crutch for a weak calculator but what makes the strong calculator possible, the same relation the strips bear to the controller.

And then the abacus does something the flight strip never did, and it is the detail that earns the story its place in this chapter. Operators who train long enough put the instrument down. In the practice called *anzan*, expert soroban users calculate on an abacus that exists only in imagination, flicking beads that are not there, watching carries propagate across a device made of nothing, and they do it as fast as with the physical tool and sometimes faster. When the psychologist Giyoo Hatano studied the phenomenon's "grand experts," he found they could hold fifteen or sixteen digits in immediate memory where an ordinary person holds Miller's seven items, and then he found the fact that gives the finding its meaning: their span for letters, or for the names of fruits, was ordinary.[^5] The same pattern appears at the neural level in a different group of experts on the other side of the planet. London taxi drivers must pass "the Knowledge," a multi-year memorization of 25,000 streets and 20,000 landmarks, and the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire found that successfully acquiring it physically enlarges the posterior hippocampus, the brain's spatial-navigation center, with gray-matter volume increasing in proportion to years on the job. Bus drivers, who follow fixed routes and never build the full map, show no such enlargement. When the taxi drivers retire, the added gray matter recedes.[^6] The enlarged capacity was not a bigger head but the internalized instrument, a structure built outside and then practiced until it ran inside, and it held exactly what the instrument held and nothing else. *The road between the head and the world runs in both directions:* what will not fit inside is moved out onto strips and beads and paper, and an external structure rehearsed long enough is moved back in, running in imagination with the same discipline it had in wood and wire.

Once you see the maneuver you find it everywhere at the humbler end of the scale. Long division is an algorithm designed around a page; nobody's working memory carries the running remainders of a four-digit division, and the procedure exists in the form it does because the paper exists to hold what the head cannot. The mathematician's blackboard and the carpenter's pencil line on the board he is about to cut are the same organ: not records made after the thinking, but the medium the thinking is done in.

So much for the outside half. The inside half is the head's own notation, and for most people, most of the time, that notation is words. The running stream of silent speech that names what one is currently thinking about is the inner monologue, and it is not a literary conceit but a measurable phenomenon: when the psychologist Russell Hurlburt equipped people with beepers and trained them to report exactly what was in their experience at the instant of the tone, inner speech turned out to occupy a large share of waking cognition, and, just as importantly, the share varied enormously from person to person.[^7] Some people live inside a nearly continuous narration. Others, a substantial minority, report little or none, a condition that acquired a name, *anendophasia*, only in 2024, when researchers showed the difference was behaviorally real: people with little inner speech performed measurably worse on verbal working-memory tasks and on rhyme judgments, tasks where silently saying the words is the natural strategy.

Here a tempting inference should be named and resisted, because it comes up whenever the finding is described: if the voice is missing, is the intelligence less? The data refuse the conclusion. The deficits that have been demonstrated are format-specific, confined to tasks where a verbal notation is the tool of choice, and the same studies found the low-inner-speech participants managing everything else without apparent trouble, in some cases by visibly substituting another notation, imagery where others use narration. This is Hatano's finding run in reverse. The grand experts' fifteen-digit span did not make them generally smarter, and its absence in the rest of us does not make us generally duller; it was an instrument, and it held what instruments hold. The honest summary is the one that has been implicit since the flight strips: *inner speech is not the thinking but one notation the thinking can be held in,* load-bearing for the operations that fit it, dispensable for the rest, exactly as a workspace tool should be. The workspace is the capacity, and the voice is one format it can run in.

Everything in this chapter so far has been about what the workspace holds. The harder question is what never gets into it, and why so much of the work of becoming good at anything consists of arranging that as little as possible does.

Deliberation is expensive. The currency matters, because the popular version of this claim is wrong in an instructive way.

The popular version says thinking burns calories at a spectacular rate. Chess grandmasters, the story runs, burn six thousand calories on a tournament day and shed ten pounds across a match. The figure has been in ESPN and CNBC and most of the other places a figure can get to, and it is an invention. It descends from one line in a 2009 commencement speech, which rested on a 1975 master's thesis that measured the breathing of eleven club players rated between 1329 and 1921, the highest-rated of them still nearly six hundred points short of a grandmaster, and which calculated no calories at all. The speaker took a peak breathing rate, treated it as an average, arrived at a threefold increase, multiplied two thousand calories by three, and published six thousand.[^8]

The real figure is nothing like it. The brain is about two percent of body weight and draws about twenty percent of the body's energy, and it draws that share continuously, whether you are proving a theorem or staring at the ceiling. Marcus Raichle, the neurologist whose imaging work established how much of that budget goes to ongoing activity rather than to any task, put the point plainly: the cost is overwhelmingly fixed. Hard thinking moves the needle, and it moves it a little.[^9]

So why does a grandmaster lose weight at a tournament? Because of everything except the thinking. Heart rate, reported above 170 in a strong player at a critical position. Blood pressure. Respiration. The whole sympathetic apparatus running for five hours against a clock and an opponent who is working for your failure, followed by broken sleep and a suppressed appetite. That is an athlete's cost and it is perfectly real. It simply isn't the cost of computation, which is why the case doesn't carry to the mathematician alone at a desk with no clock and no adversary, working just as hard and burning very little.

Which leaves the question of why thinking feels so expensive when the fuel bill is so small.

The feeling isn't a fuel gauge. Robert Kurzban and his colleagues at Pennsylvania argued, in the paper that reframed the question, that the subjective sense of mental effort is the felt output of a cost-benefit computation: a running estimate of what the mechanisms now occupied by this task could be doing instead. Effort feels bad in order to make you reallocate. It is a control signal rather than a readout of depletion, and on that account the reluctance you feel in front of a hard problem isn't weakness, and isn't even a judgment you made. It's the machinery quoting you the price of the workspace.[^10]

And the price is the one this chapter opened with. The workspace holds about seven things. While it holds these, it holds nothing else, and there is no second workspace to put the remainder in. That is the entire scarcity. It is a scarcity of a tier, not of a fuel.

A metabolic cost does exist, and when it was finally measured it proved specific rather than spectacular. A team at the Paris Brain Institute put people through a full day of demanding cognitive work and found glutamate accumulating in the lateral prefrontal cortex, alongside a measurable drift in their choices: smaller rewards, shorter delays, options that needed less evaluating. A low-cost bias, arriving by late afternoon.[^11] Which is this section's argument turning up as a laboratory result. Sustained deliberation leaves a residue, the residue makes deliberation dearer still, and the system answers by moving down the rungs.

So the aversion is correct, and it is more than correct. It is the mechanism working as designed. What separates one practitioner from another isn't appetite for thought. It's how much of the domain has crystallized below the level where thought is needed.

The knowledge hasn't gone anywhere down there. It's doing the same work in a cheaper form. There are several such forms, they are not interchangeable, and they fail in characteristically different ways.

At the bottom is *instinct*: an unlearned disposition toward a whole pattern of behavior, arriving with the animal, complete, needing no example. The greylag goose who finds an egg outside the nest stretches her neck and rolls it back with the underside of her bill, and if you take the egg away mid-motion she finishes the motion anyway, shepherding nothing across the ground to a nest she has already reached. Niko Tinbergen, the Dutch ethologist who shared a Nobel for working out how such behaviors are assembled, found oystercatchers that would abandon their own clutch to straddle a plaster egg several times too large, because the rule says bigger is better and nothing in the world that shaped the rule had ever tested the top of that scale.[^12] The behavior isn't stupid. It's exactly right for the world it was fitted to, and it has no way to represent a different one.

Instinct, at the scale of a whole animal, stopped being a matter of scattered examples in 2024, and the moment had been a long time coming. The project of mapping every connection in a brain had known its goal since the 1980s, when it finished the worm (three hundred and two neurons, the whole of it) and had spent the four decades since trying to scale that up to an animal with a real brain, without quite getting there. Then a consortium published the fruit fly, and the reach connected. The first complete wiring diagram of an adult brain, all of it: roughly a hundred and forty thousand neurons and fifty million synapses, every connection between them mapped, the whole thing reading like Tinbergen's list made complete.[^13] The experiment that matters to this chapter appeared in the same issue. Another group took the wiring and ran it as a computer model of the whole brain, and the model did fly things without being taught any of them. Give it the taste of sugar or water and the neurons that begin feeding light up in the predicted order, and the motor neurons that drive the feeding response fire; touch the antenna and the grooming circuit wakes. The researchers then confirmed, in the living animal, that the predictions were right. The behavior came out of the wiring alone, which is the definition of the bottom rung. A third paper in the same issue put the point as a structural fact: read causally, the fly's brain resolves into a large collection of small circuits that operate largely independently: reflex modules, each complete, each blind to everything outside its own wiring. That is instinct with the mystery removed, hardwired knowledge in its purest form, and the first time in history a whole brain could be read off the page.

Just above it, and constantly confused with it, is the *automatic reflex*: an unlearned arc with no pattern behind it at all. The knee that jerks. The eye that blinks. The posture that corrects itself before anything has been noticed. Flying insects hold their backs toward the brightest part of the visual field, which under an open sky is the sky, and which therefore keeps them level. The response has nothing to do with navigation and nothing to do with being drawn toward light. Put a lamp in the field and the same reflex, firing correctly, banks the insect into a standing turn around the lamp until it stalls and drops.[^14] Nothing malfunctioned. A stabilizer calibrated against a source at infinity met one a foot away, and the geometry did the rest. The moth at the candle is the clearest picture we have of a cheap layer running exactly to specification, at full confidence, in a world it was never specified for, with no channel by which the news could reach it.

Above the unlearned rungs, and different in kind because it can be built and rebuilt, is the *trained reflex*. The braking foot. The typist's fingers. The block that lands before the strike has been recognized as a strike, the hand that finds the right strip without the controller looking at the rack. It's manufactured by repetition against one particular distribution, it runs below the level where it can be watched, and it's as blind to the edges of its domain as the innate rungs are. A driver of thirty years' standing, arriving in Britain, doesn't edge into the wrong lane wondering whether he has it right. He enters it fluently, instantly, without a flicker of hesitation, because fluency is what got trained, and fluency carries no information about whether the road agrees.

Above that is the rung this chapter has been circling since the abacus, and the old languages have a better word for it than English does. English reaches for *groove*, which is serviceable and says the main thing: the knowledge has become a channel the work runs in rather than an object the work is done to. The Greek is *hexis*, a settled condition built up by practice. It is the word standing behind the phrase "by reason of use" in the passage from Hebrews that {{c:id00107}} takes up, where the mature reader's organs of perception have been athletically trained toward discernment. The abacus master flicking beads that aren't there is in *hexis*. So is the taxi driver whose posterior hippocampus thickened around the map of London. So, above all, is the skald composing in *dróttkvætt* at speed, in the hall, in front of the man the poem is about.

One distinction keeps this rung from being a very good reflex, and the next chapter depends on it. A reflex returns the response it was drilled to return and composes nothing, which is precisely its virtue. A *hexis* composes. The skald isn't reaching for a stored line; the meter, absorbed long past the point of being consulted, has already pruned the space of possible lines, so that what arrives at deliberation is a short list that scans. This is what {{c:id00104}} means, literally, when it says structure is a generator. The constraint has stopped costing and started paying.

And at the top, *thinking*: slow, small, costly, general, and the only rung that can entertain the possibility that it is the wrong rung.

Read the five in order and one property tracks the cost, and it is the property this whole book is about. The cheaper the rung, the less able it is to detect its own inapplicability. The goose can't represent an absent egg. The insect can't represent a light at a finite distance. The driver can't feel that the road has changed sides, because feeling isn't what got trained. *Hexis* is the first rung with any purchase on the question, and the purchase runs only as far as the domain that built it: the skald hears a false line as a wrong note, and hears nothing at all when the material isn't verse. Only deliberation can notice that it is the wrong instrument for the job, and it notices slowly, unreliably, and at the price that made every rung below it worth building.

The limiting case of that property is not a physical world at all. It is another person, because there what the cheap rung cannot represent is not an absent egg or a lamp at a finite distance but an absent belief, and a belief leaves no mark on the visual field. {{c:id00101}} described the faculty that does this work and the odd position it occupies, modeling what it is never permitted to inspect. What belongs here is that the work runs on two rungs rather than one, and that they differ from each other as much as anything else on this ladder. There is a fast version, the one that winces when somebody else touches the stove, running below report, arriving already evaluated, costing nothing. It is excellent across the distribution that trained it and blind at the edges in precisely the way the ladder predicts, which is why a confident misreading of a stranger raised somewhere else feels, from the inside, exactly like a correct reading of an old friend. And there is a slow version that builds an explicit model of the other party offline, in words, by running hypotheticals and checking them against what happened: the version a negotiator uses the night before, and the version anyone uses on a person they have already got badly wrong once. The second is not the first done better. It is a different rung, with its own cost and its own failure mode, and the fast one has no way of reporting that it is the wrong rung to be running.[^mentalizing]

Which sets the difficulty, because the obvious remedy is the wrong one. If the fast rungs are blind, deliberate more. But deliberation doesn't merely cost more; it performs worse at the work the lower rungs have taken over. The psychologist Sian Beilock, who studies how skilled performance comes apart, had experienced golfers putt while attending to the components of their own stroke, and the attention that helps a novice degraded the expert, whose stroke left the workspace years ago and is damaged by being hauled back into it. Splitting that same expert's attention with an unrelated task, which ought to have hurt more, hurt him less.[^15]

So the rungs are not ranked worst to best. They are specializations, each one better than its neighbors over a particular stretch of ground, and the question is never which rung is superior but which rung has the problem in front of it.

That is the routing problem, and it is hard because neither side can solve it alone. A fast rung can't tell you it's the wrong rung, for the reason just given. Deliberation could tell you, but only by deliberating, which is the whole expense the fast rung existed to avoid; a system that checks every reflex by thinking about it has no reflexes. The choice has to be made by something that is neither: cheap, structural, running ahead of the work rather than after it, reading the surface of the problem rather than its content. {{c:id00107}} takes up the faculty that does this in a person, and what becomes of a mind where it is missing. What belongs here is that the problem isn't ours alone.[^16]

Which brings the chapter to the machine, because in July of 2026 the machine's fast tier became visible. A group of researchers at Anthropic published a method for reading the concepts a language model holds in mind but does not say.[^17] Their technique, the Jacobian lens, computes at each point in the model's processing the words that activation is disposed to make the model utter later, averaged over many contexts, so that it surfaces what the model is poised to say rather than what it happens to be saying at that instant. Applied across the model's layers, it exposes a small, privileged pool of internal representations the model can report on, hold deliberately in mind, reason with, and route to whatever the task requires, surrounded by a far larger volume of automatic processing the model neither reports nor can reach. They named the pool the J-space, and argued that it behaves like a *global workspace*, the structure that the cognitive scientist Bernard Baars proposed, in an influential theory of human consciousness, as the place a thought must reach to become consciously available: a shared stage that many specialized processes can read from, as against the far larger backstage of processing that never reaches awareness.

Attend to the format of what the lens finds. It reads *verbalizable* content, words the model is disposed to say but has not said; the workspace it maps is verbal to the core, a working memory whose native and, as far as the instrument can see, only notation is words. The model sits at the far pole from anendophasia, a mind whose inner life is all monologue, which is perhaps what one should expect of a system grown entirely inside language, and it makes the human comparison precise rather than decorative: we are comparing notations of the same tier, not metaphors.

And the machine externalizes, in exactly the controllers' sense. The same researchers found that when a problem requires more steps than the model's fixed internal depth can chain together, the model writes its intermediate results into the running text as ordinary tokens and reads them back later, a comparison the authors themselves drew to a human working on scratch paper. The test that proves the strips analogy is the ablation: a model made to work a problem out on the page is far more resistant to having its internal workspace suppressed than one made to answer in a single step, because the load has been moved somewhere the suppression cannot reach, which is precisely why the controller's board survives the controller's sneeze. Chain-of-thought prompting, seen from inside, is a flight strip: reasoning moved onto a medium where it persists, where a failure of attention cannot erase it, and where, unlike the daydream it replaces, someone else can inspect it.

No one designed either half. The internal pool emerged under training pressure, and the offloading to tokens is improvised, an unforced habit the model falls into when its depth runs out, and the two facts together are this book's thesis run as an experiment. The claim has never been that the layers must be installed by hand; it is that a brain ships with built-in abilities and that intelligence becomes reliable by amplifying them with external structure, and a learning system left to its own devices has now grown the internal ability and reached for the external amplification, the same maneuver for the same limitation. What the improvisation still lacks is the controllers' discipline, the strip rather than the daydream: an external medium structured so that the verification layer of {{c:id00106}} can inspect it and the watching mind of {{c:id00107}} can check it against the reasoning it was meant to encode. The second appendix takes up what that discipline would look like in silicon. The layer itself was always in the stack; it simply took a machine, of all things, to make the invisible tier visible.

In October of 2026 a group installed both halves on purpose, in the setting least friendly to the attempt. Stratego is a game of perfect recall and radically imperfect information: both armies are laid out face down, a piece is identified only when it is made to fight, and a player's whole task is to act well on a board most of which he cannot see. The hidden pieces can be arranged in more than ten to the sixty-sixth ways, which puts the game beyond the search methods that settled chess and beyond those that settled poker, and it had stayed beyond them. A system called Ataraxos, built by Samuel Sokota and Gabriele Farina with four colleagues at Carnegie Mellon, New York University, Stanford, and MIT, beat Pim Niemeijer, the most decorated Stratego player in history, by fifteen games to one with four drawn.[^ataraxos]

The architecture is this chapter's two halves, named and kept separate. One half is a blueprint learned by self-play, and it is *hexis* in the sense developed earlier: the game absorbed so far past the point of being consulted that it arrives as a disposition, pruning the space of candidate moves before anything slower sees them. The other half runs at the moment of choice. A generative model proposes what the opponent's hidden pieces probably are, spreading probability across the arrangements consistent with everything the game has revealed, and the system then looks ahead through those possibilities before committing. The blueprint supplies the short list that scans. The decision-time machinery does what deliberation does, which is to represent what is not there.

The cost is the part this book has been waiting for, and it belongs in the first line of any honest summary. Ataraxos was trained in one week on sixteen H100 GPUs for less than eight thousand dollars. Measured against DeepNash, the 2022 system that reached expert human level on the same game, that is roughly a five-hundredth of the compute, a thirtieth of the self-play games, and a hundredth of the training examples, for strictly greater playing strength. A game that had held out against the largest machines in the world went down to a rack a university lab can rent for the price of a used car. The gain did not come from a bigger pattern matcher. It came from a smaller one with a page in front of it, and that ratio is what this book's central claim looks like once somebody prices it.

Two cautions, and both make the result more useful rather than less. The first is that the pure-pattern route had already been tried on this game and had stopped short of the top. DeepNash was built to approach an unexploitable strategy by learning alone, deliberately without search, and that was the claim made for it at the time; it reached the level of a strong human and stayed there. So the lesson is not that hidden information turns out to suit pattern matching after all. It is that pattern matching turned out to be the right instrument for one particular job inside the problem, namely forming the prior over what cannot be seen, which is exactly what a trained disposition should be good at, and the wrong instrument for the job of acting on that prior, which went to deliberation. Neither rung arrived alone. What arrived was the pairing, with the division of labour between them specified rather than left to improvisation.

The second caution is about what has and has not been done, and this paper makes it narrower than it was. Ataraxos infers what an opponent is holding, and an equilibrium strategy is in one sense built so as not to need more: play unexploitably and it ceases to matter what the other side thinks of you, which settles the regress by buying it out rather than by running it. But the same parts were turned on Hanabi and on dou dizhu, which are not adversarial in that way. Hanabi is cooperative and almost nothing else. The whole game is working out what a partner will infer from a move not yet made, and it has served the field for years as the standard test for precisely the recursive capacity that Stratego lets a player sidestep. One architecture covering the adversarial case, the cooperative case, and the team case is the first real evidence for the position taken in {{c:id00101}}, that state inference, recursive mentalizing, and self-modeling are one thing seen from three angles. It is evidence and not a demonstration, and the next several years will settle it. What can be said now, with no forecasting at all, is smaller and still worth saying. A blueprint cannot represent what it cannot see, any more than Tinbergen's goose can represent an egg that is no longer under her. The generative model is an organ built for nothing else, and it was fitted to the fast rung deliberately, by people who had measured what the fast rung could not do by itself.

Within the same year, parts of the field began building the rung below it on purpose. The observation driving the work is that a great deal of what production systems ask a language model to do isn't thinking at all. Which queue does this ticket belong in? Is this message an attack? How urgent is it, on a four-point scale? Those are reflex questions, and answering them by generating prose costs a second or two and real money and yields an answer somebody then has to parse back out of the prose. The alternative, now built several times over, is a small bidirectional model that reads the whole input in a single pass and emits a probability distribution over options fixed in advance, in about thirty milliseconds, generating no text at all. It cannot fabricate a category that doesn't exist, in the strong sense that it holds no representation in which it could. Constraint doing the work supervision was doing badly, again. What a layer like that cannot do is notice when the problem in front of it isn't one of its own, and the measurements of that failure are sharp enough to have earned a section of their own. {{c:id00107}} takes them up.[^18]

The story can now turn outward. The next chapter is about what happens when you need to transmit knowledge not just across time but across minds, across generations of readers, each with their own biases, each with their own lossy compression. The body grounds one intelligence and the notebook and the workspace keep its reasoning honest, but what about a civilization of intelligences, passing knowledge forward across centuries? What encoding survives that journey?

The skalds knew.
