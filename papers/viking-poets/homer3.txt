Sanskrit-Greek one-to-one mapping



This is well-established in comparative linguistics but the implications are usually not followed to their conclusion. The correspondences are systematic:



Sanskrit pitar = Greek patēr = Latin pater (father)

Sanskrit bhrātar = Greek phrātēr = Latin frater (brother)

Sanskrit deva = Latin deus = Old Norse tívar (gods)

Sanskrit raja = Latin rex = Gothic reiks (king)



The conventional explanation: both Sanskrit and Greek descend from PIE, and the consonant shifts between them are regular. Sanskrit preserves the PIE voiced aspirates (bh, dh, gh) which Greek converts to unvoiced aspirates (ph, th, kh). This is well-mapped and uncontroversial.



But if Hebrew/Semitic vocabulary entered the PIE stream through early contact (whether pre-Scythian or through the Scythian channel), then some of these "PIE" roots could be Semitic in origin, processed through two sets of sound changes — first the Semitic-to-PIE adaptation, then the PIE-to-daughter-language shifts. Each step is regular, so the Semitic origin becomes invisible unless you strip back through both layers of sound change to check the consonantal skeleton against Hebrew.



Karen Thompson's work on recovering Rigvedic meaning through comparison with Avestan and other early Iranian sources would benefit from checking Semitic parallels for the verses that resist Indo-European interpretation. If the earliest Vedic composers included or interacted with Saka populations who carried Semitic vocabulary, some "untranslatable" Vedic passages might yield to Hebrew comparison where Sanskrit and Avestan comparison has failed. How Herodotus handles foreign names



Herodotus renders the Gettae as Γέται (Getai) in Ionic Greek. His transliteration conventions for Near Eastern names are fairly consistent: Hebrew/Semitic gimel (ג) → Greek gamma (Γ), Hebrew tet (ט) or tav (ת) → Greek tau (τ). When he hears an emphatic consonant like tet (ט), which Greek lacks, he tends to render it as a doubled consonant — ττ — to capture the emphatic quality. So Γέτ-ται could represent a Hebrew word with ט rather than ת.



This opens up several candidates beyond Gad:



1. גט (get) — bill of divorcement



This is potentially the strongest and I'm surprised it didn't occur to me earlier. A get (גט) is the legal document of divorce. Hosea's entire prophetic framework for the northern kingdom is a divorce metaphor — God as husband, Israel as unfaithful wife, the exile as divorce proceedings. Hosea 2:4 (2:2 in English): "Plead with your mother, plead, for she is not my wife, and I am not her husband" — that's a divorce declaration.



If the exiled tribes came to be known — or to know themselves — as "the divorced ones," the term would be gittim (גיטים) or simply gittai (גיטי) in a construct form meaning "those of the divorce/separation." Herodotus hearing gittai would render it Γέται perfectly. The doubled tau captures the emphatic tet of גט.



The semantic logic is devastating: they are literally "the divorced" — the people whom God put away. They carry their theological status as their tribal name. And they don't even know it anymore, because they've forgotten the language in which their name is a legal term.



And there's a geographic echo: Gath (גת), one of the five Philistine cities, is the same consonantal root. David fled to Gath and lived among the Philistines — an Israelite hiding among foreigners, taking on their identity. The pattern repeats.



2. גת (gat) — winepress



Same consonants as get, different word. A gat (גת) is a winepress — as in Gethsemane (גת שמנים, "press of oils"). This matters because the Gettae were famous in the ancient world specifically for their wine culture. Herodotus and other Greek sources describe the Thracians and Gettae as prodigious wine drinkers and cultivators. The Gettae's neighbor-relatives the Thracians were associated with Dionysus — the wine god.



If the Gettae were named for their winepress culture, that's interesting because viticulture was a defining Israelite agricultural practice. Noah plants a vineyard immediately after the flood. The spies bring back grapes from Canaan. Isaiah 5's "Song of the Vineyard" uses the gat (winepress) as a central metaphor for Israel itself — "he built a watchtower in the midst of it, and also made a gat in it" (Isaiah 5:2). Israel is the vineyard with the winepress. A people who carried viticulture from Israel into the Pontic steppe and became known for it could easily acquire "winepress people" as their ethnonym.



The Gettae region (modern Romania/Bulgaria) remains one of Europe's oldest wine-producing areas. The continuity of viticulture in that specific territory from the Gettae period through Roman Dacia to the present is well-documented archaeologically.



3. גדי (gedi) — young goat, kid



As in Ein Gedi (עין גדי, "spring of the kid"). The consonantal skeleton g-d-i → Get-ai is clean, with the standard d→t devoicing. A pastoral people named for their goat-herding would be unremarkable among steppe nomads. But the goat has a specific resonance in Israelite theology — the scapegoat (שעיר לעזאזל, sa'ir la-azazel) of Yom Kippur carries the sins of the nation into the wilderness. A people "sent away" into the wilderness carrying the nation's sins — that's another metaphor for the exile.



This is weaker than get but the goat-pastoralism connection has archaeological support — Scythian/Gettae material culture shows extensive goat-herding.



4. גר (ger) — stranger, sojourner, resident alien



The ger in Torah law is the foreigner living among Israel — the one who is "with you" but not fully "of you." Abraham describes himself as a ger v'toshav (גר ותושב, "stranger and sojourner") in Genesis 23:4. If the exiled tribes understood themselves as having become gerim — strangers, sojourners in foreign lands — the term could crystallize into an ethnonym. Gerim → Geri/Getai requires an r→t shift, which is less standard but not impossible in certain transmission environments (lateral and dental consonants interchange in some language contact situations).



This one is phonologically weaker but semantically strong — a people defined by their condition of permanent foreignness, never fully belonging to any land they inhabit. Which is, again, exactly the prophetic description of exiled Israel.



5. גדר (gader) — wall, fence, enclosure



A gader is a boundary wall or enclosure. A geder is also used metaphorically for boundary or separation. If we take g-d-r and drop the resh (which does happen in name-shortening), we get g-d → Get. Weaker phonologically, but the semantic field of "boundary people" or "people of the wall/enclosure" could describe a people who maintained distinct boundaries between themselves and surrounding populations — which is exactly the Gettae's reputation among the Greeks.
